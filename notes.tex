\chapter{Lecture 1 -- 07/09/2026}

To manage a business, we first need to understand what a business is.

A business differs from economics: economics is the social science that studies how scarce resources are allocated, whereas a business is an organization that produces goods or services to satisfy customer needs and generate a profit.

Another perspective views a business as a legal entity. As an organization, it operates within a hierarchy that includes roles such as CEOs, managers, and employees.

\subsection{Law}

Law can be defined as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A set of rules designed to resolve conflicts of interest.
    \item An organizational framework to enforce those rules.
    \item A system of shared beliefs and social norms.
\end{itemize}

Generally, starting a business in a jurisdiction with strong legal protections is advantageous, as the law safeguards capital and intellectual property from theft or infringement.

For example, Artificial Intelligence (AI) regulation varies significantly by country. What is legal in one jurisdiction may be restricted or illegal in another, requiring founders to assess legal environments prior to launch.

\subsection{Stakeholders}

While shareholders hold equity shares and own part of the company, stakeholders include any individual or entity affected by the company's decisions and operations.

There are two primary types of stakeholders:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Internal Stakeholders:} Individuals within the organization, such as employees, managers, and owners.
    \item \textbf{External Stakeholders:} External entities affected by operations, including customers, suppliers, competitors, and regulatory bodies.
\end{itemize}

Each stakeholder group utilizes distinct legal and contractual mechanisms to protect their interests.

\subsection{Financing the Company}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Debt vs. Equity:} Debt refers to borrowed capital (e.g., from banks) that must be repaid with interest. Equity refers to capital raised from investors in exchange for ownership shares.
    \item \textbf{Equity Investors:} Investors provide capital in exchange for ownership shares, seeking long-term growth and accepting business risk. Key types include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Business Angels:} High-net-worth individuals who invest their personal capital in early-stage startups.
        \item \textbf{Venture Capitalists (VCs):} Professional investment firms that deploy capital from institutional funds.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Debt Financing Instruments:} Different debt instruments carry distinct risk profiles and terms:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Bonds:} Long-term debt securities issued to investors that pay periodic interest.
        \item \textbf{Loans:} Direct debt agreements with fixed repayment schedules and interest rates.
        \item \textbf{Lines of Credit:} Flexible credit arrangements allowing companies to draw funds up to a set limit as needed.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Business Model}

A business model is a conceptual framework describing how an organization creates, delivers, and captures value.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Revenue Assumptions:} What a company charges for (e.g., direct sales, subscription fees, data monetization, advertising space).
    \item \textbf{Cost Assumptions:} What a company incurs expenses for (e.g., raw materials, labor, marketing, R\&D).
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}[Business Model Definition]
A business model defines who your target customer is, what value you create or add for them, and how you deliver that value at a sustainable cost.
\end{keybox}

The foundational step in business modeling is identifying customer needs. Sustainable profitability—rather than revenue alone—is essential, as profits generate the capital required for reinvestment and growth.

\textbf{Example (Nespresso):} Nespresso tested several business models that initially failed. They achieved success by repositioning coffee as a luxury experience—opening boutiques in premium retail locations worldwide and selling single-serve pods at a premium price point.

A robust business model also serves as a diagnostic tool to identify operational and legal risks.

\begin{quote}
\textit{``A business model describes how the business runs, whereas a competitive strategy defines how the business performs better than its competitors.''}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Emerging Technologies}

An emerging technology is a novel, rapidly growing technology characterized by fast development cycles and high market uncertainty.

Multiple technological solutions can address the same underlying customer need. For example, to commute to school, a person might buy a bicycle, rent one, walk, or hire a ride-sharing service. The optimal choice depends on constraints such as available budget and time.

\subsection{Examples of Business Models}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Crowdsourcing:} Outsourcing tasks or content creation to a distributed group of individuals, typically via online platforms (e.g., Wikipedia, YouTube).
    \item \textbf{Freemium:} Offering a basic version of a product or service for free while charging for premium features or higher usage tiers (e.g., Dropbox).
    \item \textbf{Subscription:} Charging customers a recurring fee (monthly or annually) for continuous access to a product or service (e.g., Netflix).
    \item \textbf{Negative Operating Cycle:} Receiving payment from customers before paying suppliers for goods, optimizing working capital (e.g., Amazon).
    \item \textbf{Disintermediation:} Removing intermediaries from the supply chain to sell directly to end consumers, reducing transaction costs (e.g., Dell).
    \item \textbf{Aggregator:} Consolidating information or service listings from multiple providers into a single platform for customer convenience (e.g., Airbnb).
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Lecture 2 -- 09/09/2026 Business Model Canvas}


The \textbf{Business Model Canvas (BMC)} is a visual tool used to describe, analyze, and develop a company's business model. It represents the main elements of a business and shows how they are connected.

The Business Model Canvas describes four main areas of a business:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Customers}: who the company serves;
    \item \textbf{Value}: what value the company provides to customers;
    \item \textbf{Infrastructure}: how the company creates and delivers value;
    \item \textbf{Finances}: how the company generates revenues and manages costs.
\end{itemize}

The Business Model Canvas helps entrepreneurs and managers visualize the business model, identify relationships between its different elements, and understand possible trade-offs.

\subsection{Business Model vs. Business Plan}

A \textbf{business model} describes how a company creates, delivers, and captures value. In particular, it explains how the company operates and how it generates profit.

A \textbf{business plan}, instead, is a formal document that describes the company's objectives, strategies, market analysis, operations, and expected financial performance.

Therefore:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Business model}: explains \emph{how the business works};
    \item \textbf{Business plan}: explains \emph{how the business will be developed and managed}.
\end{itemize}

The Business Model Canvas is a tool that helps entrepreneurs and managers visualize and analyze the business model.

\section{Business Model Examples}

A company can generate revenues through different business models.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{product:}The company sells a product or service directly to customers, generating revenue through direct sales.
    \item \textbf{publicity / advertising:} The company generates revenue by selling advertising space or promoting products and services to a target audience.
    \item \textbf{fee / usage fee:} The company provides a service or platform and charges users a fee for access or for using the service. The customer pays according to the amount or frequency of usage.
    \item \textbf{subscription:} The company charges customers a recurring fee, usually monthly or annually, in exchange for continuous access to a product or service.
    \item \textbf{freemium:} The company offers a basic version of the product or service for free, while charging customers for additional or premium features. The objective is to attract a large number of users with the free version and convert some of them into paying customers.
    \item \textbf{accessories:} The company sells additional products or services that complement the main offering. Revenues are generated through \textbf{upselling} or \text bf{cross-selling} of accessories.
\end{itemize}

\section{1. Value Proposition}

The \textbf{value proposition} explains the value that a company offers to its customers and why customers should choose its product or service instead of competitors' alternatives.

The value proposition can be based on different factors, such as:
price, quality, convenience, innovation, performance, customization, design, brand, accessibility, and risk reduction.


A company can have \textbf{several value propositions} because different customer segments may have different needs and expectations.

The company should identify the value proposition that is the most profitable and sustainable for each target customer segment.

\subsection{Types of Value Propositions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Novelty: The product or service is new and innovative, offering a unique solution to a problem or need.
    \item Performance: The product or service offers superior performance or functionality compared with competitors.
    \item Customization: The product or service can be tailored to meet the specific needs or preferences of individual customers.
    \item Design: The product or service has an appealing aesthetic or user experience that differentiates it from competitors.
    \item Brand / Status: The product or service is associated with a prestigious brand or represents a status symbol that increases its perceived value.
    \item Price: The product or service is offered at a competitive or affordable price that appeals to cost-conscious customers.   
    \item Convenience: The product or service is easy to access, use, or integrate into customers' lives, saving time or effort.
    \item Risk Reduction: The product or service reduces uncertainty or potential negative outcomes for customers,
    \item Accessibility: The product or service is made available to a wider range of customers, including customers with limited resources or special needs.
    \item Cost Reduction: The product or service helps customers save money or reduce expenses in the long term.
\end{itemize}


\section{2. Customer Segments (For Whom?)}

The \textbf{customer segment} is the group of people or organizations that a company aims to serve and for whom it creates value.

The market is divided into different groups of customers. These groups can have different needs, preferences, behaviors, and characteristics.

A segment can be identified according to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Demographics}: age, gender, income, education, occupation, etc.;
    \item \textbf{Psychographics}: lifestyle, personality, values, interests, etc.;
    \item \textbf{Behavior}: purchasing habits, frequency of use, brand loyalty, etc.;
    \item \textbf{Needs}: specific problems or requirements that customers want to solve.
\end{itemize}

A \textbf{market segment} is a group of people who respond in a similar way to a marketing strategy.

For example, engineering students and architecture students may represent different customer segments because they can have different needs and preferences.

The customer segment can also be divided into \textbf{sub-segments} based on specific characteristics or behaviors.

In a market, there are different customer groups, and the company chooses a \textbf{target segment} on which to focus.

The \textbf{target segment} is the group of customers that the company specifically aims to serve and create value for.

\subsection{Example: Ferrari}

\textbf{Ferrari} focuses strongly on quality, performance, luxury, exclusivity, and brand/status.

Its target customers are generally high-income individuals who value: high performance, luxury, exclusivity, prestige, and status.

The target segment can also be divided into smaller sub-segments according to customers' specific preferences and motivations.

For Ferrari, customers may be particularly interested in the \textbf{status and prestige} associated with owning the brand.

\section{3. Channels(  How Do We Get It to Them?)}

\textbf{Channels} describe how a company communicates with customers and how it delivers its value proposition to them.

Channels can be:
\begin{itemize}
    \item direct or indirect;
    \item online or offline;
    \item owned by the company or shared with other companies.
\end{itemize}

Examples of channels include: physical stores, websites, mobile applications, social media, email marketing, distributors, partners, marketplaces, events, traditional advertising, and print media.

The company has to choose the channel that is the most \textbf{effective and efficient} for each customer segment.

\subsection{Direct Channels}

A \textbf{direct channel} is a channel through which the company sells or communicates directly with customers.

Examples include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a physical store owned by the company;
    \item the company's website;
    \item the company's mobile application;
    \item direct sales.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Indirect Channels}

An \textbf{indirect channel} involves an intermediary between the company and the final customer.

Examples include: distributors, retailers, agents, business partners, and marketplaces.

\subsection{Retail Store Example}

A \textbf{retail store} is a business that sells products directly to customers.

The retailer can buy products from another company and resell them to customers, generating revenue through a \textbf{markup} or commission.

For example:

\[
\text{Purchase Price} + \text{Markup} = \text{Selling Price}
\]

The retail store can be: physical, online, or both.

A retailer can also provide additional services such as: customer support, returns, exchanges, warranties, marketing and promotional activities, and loyalty programs.

The channels and customer relationships should be adapted to the specific needs and characteristics of the target segment.

\section{4. Customer Relationships}

\textbf{Customer relationships} describe the type of relationship that a company establishes and maintains with its customers.

Customer relationships can be:

\begin{itemize}
    \item personal or automated;
    \item transactional or long-term.
\end{itemize}

Examples include: personal assistance, dedicated personal assistance, self-service, automated services, loyalty programs, feedback systems, communities, and co-creation.

The company has to design the customer relationship that is most suitable for each customer segment.

For example, a cleaning-room worker and a university teacher may have very different needs and preferences. Therefore, the company may need to use different channels and customer relationships for different customer segments.

\section{5. Revenue Streams}

\textbf{Revenue streams} are the sources of income that a company generates from its business activities.

They explain \textbf{how the company makes money}.

Revenue streams can be based on different pricing models, such as:  one-time sales, recurring subscriptions, licensing fees, advertising revenue, usage fees, and commissions.


Different customer segments or different value propositions can generate different revenue streams.

For example:

\[
\text{Revenue} = \text{Price} \times \text{Quantity Sold}
\]

However, a company may also generate recurring revenues through subscriptions or usage-based pricing.

\section{6. Key Resources}

\textbf{Key resources} are the assets that a company needs to create, deliver, and capture value.

Key resources can be classified into four main categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical resources};
    \item \textbf{Intellectual resources};
    \item \textbf{Human resources};
    \item \textbf{Financial resources}.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Example: Electric Car Company}

To produce electric cars, a company needs different types of key resources.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical}: factories, machinery, batteries, raw materials;
    \item \textbf{Intellectual}: patents, designs, software, technology;
    \item \textbf{Human}: engineers, designers, technicians;
    \item \textbf{Financial}: capital, loans, and investments.
\end{itemize}

Key resources can be owned by the company or accessed through partnerships and outsourcing.

\section{7. Key Activities}

\textbf{Key activities} are the most important actions that a company must perform to create and deliver its value proposition, reach customers, and generate revenues.

Examples of key activities include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item production;
    \item research and development;
    \item marketing;
    \item sales;
    \item distribution;
    \item problem solving;
    \item platform management;
    \item customer support.
\end{itemize}

The key activities depend on the company's business model.

For example, for a manufacturing company, production may be one of the most important activities. For a technology company, software development and research may be more important.

\section{8. Key Partnerships}

\textbf{Key partnerships} are strategic alliances that a company forms with other organizations in order to improve its capabilities, reduce risks, access resources, or reach new markets.

Key partners can include: suppliers, distributors, technology providers, research institutions, joint-venture partners, and other companies.


These partnerships can help the company ensure a reliable supply chain, efficient charging infrastructure, and advanced vehicle features.

A company can start without key partnerships, but partnerships can become important when the company needs external resources, expertise, technology, distribution, or risk sharing.

\subsection{Four Types of Partnerships}

There are four main types of partnerships:

\subsubsection{1. Strategic Alliances Between Non-Competitors}

Two companies that operate in different industries or markets collaborate to achieve mutual benefits.

They are not direct competitors but can complement each other's activities.

\subsubsection{2. Coopetition: Strategic Partnerships Between Competitors}

Two companies that operate in the same industry or market collaborate to achieve common goals.

Examples include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item joint research and development;
    \item shared marketing campaigns;
    \item co-branding;
    \item technology development.
\end{itemize}

This type of relationship can be described as \textbf{coopetition}, because companies cooperate while still competing in other areas.

\subsubsection{3. Joint Ventures}

Two or more companies create or participate in a separate business entity to pursue a specific project or business opportunity.

The partners share resources, risks, responsibilities, and potentially profits.

\subsubsection{4. Buyer-Supplier Relationships / Bottleneck Partnerships}

A company establishes a partnership with a supplier or another organization to secure access to an essential resource or overcome a specific constraint.

\section{the two clock of accounting}
accounting has two clocks: the cash clock and the economic clock. The cash clock records transactions when cash is received or paid, while the economic clock records transactions when they are earned or incurred, regardless of when cash is exchanged. This means that revenue is recognized when it is earned, and expenses are recognized when they are incurred, even if payment has not yet been made. The accrual clock provides a more accurate picture of a company's financial performance and position, as it reflects the economic reality of transactions rather than just the timing of cash flows.

\section{Liquidity: working capital and ratios}

Cash is immediately available for payment, but other current assets may not be converted into cash quickly or at their recorded value. A profitable company can therefore experience a cash shortage.

Current assets include cash and items expected to be sold, consumed or realised in the normal operating cycle, or generally within twelve months. Current liabilities are obligations expected to be settled in that cycle or due in the short term. The twelve-month rule is a useful simplification, not the only classification criterion. Equity is not a current liability and is not subtracted when calculating working capital.

Net working capital is an amount expressed in currency:
\begin{equation*}
\boxed{\text{Working capital}=\text{Current assets}-\text{Current liabilities}.}
\end{equation*}
A positive value means current assets exceed current liabilities at the balance-sheet date. It does not ensure that cash will be available when each bill becomes due: receivables may be collected late and inventory may sell slowly.

The current ratio compares the same categories as working capital but expresses their relationship as a quotient:
\begin{equation*}
\text{Current ratio}=\frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}.
\end{equation*}
The quick ratio focuses on assets that can more readily be used to pay short-term obligations:
\begin{equation*}
\text{Quick ratio}=\frac{\text{Cash}+\text{Short-term investments}+\text{Receivables}}{\text{Current liabilities}}.
\end{equation*}
It excludes inventory and other items, such as prepaid expenses, that cannot readily pay bills. An alternative simplified numerator is current assets minus inventory and prepayments, when those categories explain the difference. A ratio above 1 says that the assets included in its numerator exceed current liabilities on that date; it does not guarantee timely payment. The quality of receivables, payment dates, cash flows and industry norms also matter. Neither ratio is defined in the usual way when current liabilities are zero.

\section{Solvency and indebtedness}

Solvency depends on obligations, the value and composition of assets, and the capacity to generate future cash. Merely having total assets above long-term debt is not a sufficient assessment. Borrowing can finance growth but creates payment commitments that increase risk if income or cash flows fall.

The term ``financial leverage ratio'' is ambiguous: it can refer to different measures. If the numerator includes \emph{all liabilities}, the first ratio should be labelled liabilities-to-equity:
\begin{equation*}
\text{Liabilities-to-equity}=\frac{\text{Total liabilities}}{\text{Total equity}}.
\end{equation*}
A value of 0.8 means that the company has 80 cents of liabilities for each euro of book equity. Some analysts use only interest-bearing borrowing in the numerator and call the result debt-to-equity; this is a different definition. The equity multiplier, assets divided by equity, is another leverage measure. A debt-to-equity figure is difficult to interpret in the usual way when equity is zero or negative.

If ``debt'' is defined as all liabilities, the corresponding ratio to assets is
\begin{equation*}
\text{Liabilities-to-assets}=\frac{\text{Total liabilities}}{\text{Total assets}}.
\end{equation*}
This indicates the share of book assets financed through obligations to others. If a course uses \emph{debt ratio} for interest-bearing borrowing divided by assets, that narrower definition should be stated and used consistently. A higher ratio can indicate greater financial risk, but its meaning depends on the business model, cash-flow stability and comparison group.

\section{Operating efficiency: asset turnover}

Total asset turnover measures sales generated per unit of assets during a period:
\begin{align*}
\text{Total asset turnover}&=\frac{\text{Net sales}}{\text{Average total assets}},\\
\text{Average total assets}&=\frac{\text{Opening assets}+\text{Closing assets}}{2}.
\end{align*}
A high turnover can reflect effective use of assets, but it does not show the profit margin earned on those sales. Because net sales cover a period and the balance sheet measures assets at points in time, the average of opening and closing assets is commonly used.

\section{Earnings: EBIT and EBT}

EBIT means \emph{earnings before interest and taxes}. It is an amount, not a ratio, and is commonly used to examine performance before financing cost and income tax. EBT means \emph{earnings before taxes}: it reflects interest expense and other relevant items before income tax. In a simplified income statement, EBIT minus net interest expense gives EBT, and EBT minus income tax gives net income. Actual statements can contain other non-operating items, so this sequence needs to be checked against the statement being analysed.

\section{Returns: ROI, ROA and ROE}

For a particular project, one simple definition of return on investment is
\begin{equation*}
\text{ROI}=\frac{\text{Net gain from the investment}}{\text{Cost of the investment}}\times100\%.
\end{equation*}
Net gain means proceeds or benefits minus the investment cost and any other costs included in the chosen definition. If proceeds are 120 and the investment costs 100, net gain is 20 and ROI is 20\%. Managerial accounting may instead use ``ROI'' for a segment's operating income divided by its average invested assets. Its numerator, denominator and period must therefore be specified. A simple ROI also ignores when cash flows occur.

Using net income as the measure of profit, return on assets and return on equity can be written as
\begin{align*}
\text{ROA}&=\frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Average total assets}}\times100\%,\\
\text{ROE}&=\frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Average equity}}\times100\%.
\end{align*}
The averages ordinarily use opening and closing balances. ROA measures net income relative to the assets employed; ROE measures net income relative to owners' equity. Some courses use an operating-profit version of ROA: the two versions should not be mixed in a comparison. Debt can amplify ROE and also amplify losses, so a high ROE alone does not establish that a business is stronger. ROE becomes hard to interpret when equity is very small or negative.

\section{Interpreting comparisons}

EBIT and EBT are absolute earnings amounts, while ROI, ROA and ROE are ratios. Ratios can help compare businesses of different sizes, and EBIT can also be compared across businesses when its definition and the underlying accounting are aligned. All these measures can be examined over time. Useful comparisons require compatible definitions, the same period and, generally, businesses in similar industries. No metric is automatically more useful than the others in every situation.

\section{break-even point}
the expenses can be splitted in fixed expenses and variable expenses. the fixed expenses are the expenses that do not change with the level of production or sales, such as rent, salaries, and insurance. the variable expenses are the expenses that change with the level of production or sales, such as raw materials, direct labor, and sales commissions.
the break-even point is the relationship between expenses and revenues. it is the point at which total revenues equal total expenses, resulting in neither profit nor loss. the break-even point can be calculated using the formula:
\begin{equation*}
\text{Break-even point (units)} = \frac{\text{Fixed expenses}}{\text{Selling price per unit} - \text{Variable expenses per unit}}.
\end{equation*}
for example, if a company has fixed expenses of 10,000, a selling price of 50 per unit, and variable expenses of 30 per unit, the break-even point would be:
\begin{equation*}
\text{Break-even point (units)} = \frac{10,000}{50 - 30} = \frac{10,000}{20} = 500 \text{ units}.
\end{equation*}
break-even point in term of revenue can be calculated using the formula:
\begin{equation*}
\text{Break-even point (revenue)} = \frac{\text{Fixed expenses}}{\text{Contribution margin ratio}}.
\end{equation*}

they are tools used to analyze the internal and external environment of a business. \begin{itemize}
    \item strengths: internal attributes that are helpful to achieving the objective.
    \item weaknesses: internal attributes that are harmful to achieving the objective.
    \item opportunities: external conditions that are helpful to achieving the objective.
    \item threats: external conditions that are harmful to achieving the objective.
\end{itemize}

    \item social: societal trends, demographics, and    cultural factors that can influence consumer preferences and behavior.
    \item technological: technological advancements, innovations, and disruptions that can affect the industry and business operations.

competition law, also known as antitrust law, is a set of regulations that promote fair competition and prevent anti-competitive practices in the marketplace. The main objectives of competition law are to protect consumers, encourage innovation, and ensure a level playing field for businesses. Key aspects of competition law include:
1. Prohibiting anti-competitive agreements: Competition law prohibits agreements between businesses that restrict competition, such as price-fixing, market sharing, and bid-rigging. These agreements can lead to higher prices, reduced choices, and lower quality for consumers.
2. Preventing abuse of dominant market position: Competition law prevents companies with significant market power from abusing their position to eliminate competition or exploit consumers. This includes practices such as predatory pricing, exclusive dealing, and tying arrangements.
3. Regulating mergers and acquisitions: Competition law reviews mergers and acquisitions to ensure they do not substantially lessen competition in the market. Authorities may block or impose conditions on mergers that could lead to monopolistic behavior or reduced consumer welfare.
4. Promoting consumer welfare: Competition law aims to protect consumers by ensuring they have access to a variety of products and services at competitive prices. It encourages businesses to innovate, improve quality, and offer better value to consumers.
5. Enforcement and penalties: Competition authorities investigate potential violations of competition law and can impose fines, sanctions, or other remedies on businesses found to be engaging in anti-competitive practices. This helps maintain a fair and competitive marketplace.

\chapter{Acquisitions and Mergers}

There are several distinct types of corporate acquisitions and mergers, depending on the strategy and the level of cooperation between the involved entities. The most common types are detailed below:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Friendly takeover:} This occurs when the target company's management and board of directors agree to the acquisition terms. The leadership of both firms works cooperatively to negotiate the price, perform due diligence, and support the deal through to completion.
    
    \item \textbf{Hostile takeover:} This happens when the acquiring company attempts to gain control of the target company against the explicit wishes of its management and board. The acquirer typically bypasses the board by making a tender offer directly to the shareholders or launching a proxy fight to replace the current management.
    
    \item \textbf{Reverse takeover bid (RTO):} This takes place when a private company acquires a public company (often a shell company with fewer assets but an active stock listing). This strategy allows the private company to bypass the lengthy, expensive, and complex regulatory process of going public through a traditional Initial Public Offering (IPO).
    
    \item \textbf{Backflip takeover bid:} A rare type of transaction where the acquiring company becomes a subsidiary of the purchased company after the deal is completed. Although the acquirer initiates the deal and retains operational control, the target company survives as the legal entity because it has a stronger brand identity or better corporate structure.
\end{itemize}
\section{The Due Diligence Process}
Before finalizing any transaction, the acquiring company must undergo a rigorous \textbf{due diligence process}. This is a comprehensive investigation and analysis of the target company's financial, legal, and operational status. The primary goals are to verify all material facts, identify potential hidden liabilities, and ensure that the final purchase price reflects the true value of the business.

\section{Corporate Synergies}
The strategic justification for most mergers and acquisitions is the creation of \textbf{corporate synergies}. Synergy occurs when the financial value and performance of two combined companies is greater than the sum of their individual parts ($1 + 1 = 3$). These are generally divided into two main categories:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cost Synergies:} Savings achieved by eliminating redundant operations, combining corporate departments (such as HR or IT), and gaining higher purchasing power with suppliers.
    \item \textbf{Revenue Synergies:} Increased sales generated by cross-selling products to each other's customer bases, expanding geographic reach, or combining research and development (R\&D) capabilities.
\end{itemize}

\section{Post-Merger Integration and Cultural Clash}
Even if a deal makes financial sense, many mergers fail during the integration phase due to a \textbf{cultural clash}. A cultural clash arises when two corporations with fundamentally different corporate values, management styles, communication habits, and employee behaviors try to combine. This friction often leads to lower employee morale, drop in productivity, and the loss of key talent.

\textbf{Law} is a system of rules that public authorities make and enforce to organise society and regulate behaviour.

For businesses, law defines \textbf{rights, responsibilities and limits}. It affects contracts, customers, workers, competition, data and innovation.

\hypertarget{eu-law-and-national-law}{%

\textbf{EU law} is the system of legal rules of the European Union. It includes treaties and legal acts adopted by EU institutions.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item

\item
  \textbf{Principle of conferral:} the EU can act only in areas where the treaties give it powers.
\item
  \textbf{Primacy of EU law:} when applicable EU law conflicts with national law, EU law takes priority.
\item
  Member States keep national laws, but these laws must respect their EU obligations. Their international agreements must also respect applicable EU obligations.
\item
  The \textbf{Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)} interprets EU law and helps ensure consistent application. National courts also apply EU law.
\item
  The \textbf{European Commission} can start infringement proceedings when a Member State does not meet its EU obligations.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{data-main-definitions}{%
\section{Data: Main Definitions}\label{data-main-definitions}}

\hypertarget{personal-data-and-processing}{%
\subsection{Personal data and processing}\label{personal-data-and-processing}}

\textbf{Personal data} is information about an identified or identifiable living person.

Examples include names, home addresses, ID numbers, email addresses, bank details and location data.

\textbf{Processing} means an activity involving personal data: collecting, storing, using, sharing, changing or deleting it.

\textbf{Example:} keeping a customer delivery address in a database is processing, even before the company uses it.

\hypertarget{special-categories-of-personal-data}{%
\subsection{Special categories of personal data}\label{special-categories-of-personal-data}}

Some personal data needs additional protection under \textbf{Article 9 GDPR}. Examples include health, genetic data, political opinions, religious beliefs and biometric data used to identify a person uniquely.

\textbf{Important distinctions:}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Bank details} are personal data, but are not automatically an Article 9 special category.
\item
  A normal photograph is not automatically special-category biometric data. Its technical use for unique identification matters.
\item
  Processing special-category data normally requires both an \textbf{Article 6 legal basis} and an \textbf{Article 9 exception}.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{non-personal-anonymous-and-pseudonymous-data}{%
\subsection{Non-personal, anonymous and pseudonymous data}\label{non-personal-anonymous-and-pseudonymous-data}}

\textbf{Non-personal data} does not relate to an identified or identifiable person.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Anonymous data:} people cannot reasonably be identified from it. The GDPR does not apply to genuinely anonymous data.
\item
  \textbf{Pseudonymous data:} names are replaced by codes, but people can still be identified with extra information. It remains personal data.
\item
  Removing names or grouping records does \textbf{not automatically} make data anonymous.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Example:} a customer record labelled ``Customer 481'' is still personal data if the company can connect that code to a person.

A business must identify an appropriate \textbf{legal basis} before processing personal data. The six bases under Article 6 are:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Consent:} the person agrees to a specific use.
\item
  \textbf{Contract:} processing is necessary to perform a contract or requested pre-contract steps.
\item
  \textbf{Legal obligation:} a law requires the processing.
\item
  \textbf{Vital interests:} processing is necessary to protect someone's life or another vital interest.
\item

\item

\section{Company Responsibilities}\label{company-responsibilities}}

\hypertarget{controller-and-processor}{%
\subsection{Controller and processor}\label{controller-and-processor}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Data controller:} decides why and how personal data is processed.
\item
  \textbf{Data processor:} processes personal data on the controller's behalf and instructions.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Example:} a retailer decides how to use its customer database. An external hosting provider may act as its processor.

\textbf{Outsourcing does not remove the controller's responsibilities.}

\hypertarget{main-obligations}{%
\subsection{Main obligations}\label{main-obligations}}

Companies must:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Explain processing clearly: purposes, legal basis, recipients, retention periods, international transfers and rights.
\item
  Respect \textbf{confidentiality}, including after an employment or service relationship ends where the duty continues.
\item
  Apply suitable \textbf{technical and organisational security measures}.
\item
  Assess risks and review controls when activities or threats change.
\item
  Train employees and prepare incident response procedures.
\item
  Help people exercise their rights.
\item
  Use compliant \textbf{processor contracts} and monitor suppliers appropriately.
\item
  Apply appropriate safeguards for international transfers.
\end{itemize}

A processor contract should cover instructions, confidentiality, security, assistance with rights and breaches, other processors, and return or deletion of data.

\hypertarget{risk-analysis-and-dpia}{%
\subsection{Risk analysis and DPIA}\label{risk-analysis-and-dpia}}

\textbf{Risk analysis} identifies possible threats, their likelihood and their impact. It helps the company choose suitable controls.

A \textbf{Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)} is a structured assessment required \textbf{before processing likely to create a high risk to people's rights and freedoms}.

A DPIA examines the planned processing, its necessity, its effects on people and measures to reduce the risks.

\textbf{Link:} all processing needs suitable risk management; only certain high-risk activities require a DPIA.

\hypertarget{data-protection-officer}{%
\subsection{Data Protection Officer}\label{data-protection-officer}}

A \textbf{Data Protection Officer (DPO)} advises on data protection, monitors compliance and acts as a contact for individuals and authorities.

A DPO is required in specified situations, including:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Public authorities and bodies, with an exception for courts acting in their judicial role.
\item
  Core activities involving \textbf{large-scale, regular and systematic monitoring}.
\item
  Core activities involving large-scale processing of \textbf{special-category data or criminal conviction and offence data}.
\end{itemize}

Spanish law adds requirements for certain organisations. \textbf{Not every company needs a DPO}, and appointing one does not transfer management's responsibility to that person.

\hypertarget{personal-data-breaches}{%
\subsection{Personal data breaches}\label{personal-data-breaches}}

A \textbf{personal data breach} is a security incident causing accidental or unlawful loss, destruction, alteration, disclosure of, or access to personal data.

The controller must:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Document the breach} and its effects.
\item
  Notify the authority \textbf{without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware}, unless it is unlikely to create a risk to people.
\item
  Inform affected individuals without undue delay where there is a \textbf{high risk}, unless a legal exception applies.
\end{enumerate}

A processor must inform the controller \textbf{without undue delay}. The 72-hour rule is not a rule requiring notification to every customer after every security incident.

\hypertarget{enforcement-and-penalties}{%
\subsection{Enforcement and penalties}\label{enforcement-and-penalties}}

In Spain, the \textbf{AEPD --- Agencia Española de Protección de Datos} --- is the national data protection authority. Other authorities also have specific legal responsibilities.

Authorities can issue warnings, reprimands, corrective orders, restrictions or bans on processing, and fines.

The GDPR has different fine levels. Depending on the infringement, the maximum can be \textbf{€10 million or 2\%}, or \textbf{€20 million or 4\%}, of the previous financial year's worldwide annual turnover, \textbf{whichever is higher}. These are maximum limits, not automatic fines.

\hypertarget{business-cases}{%
\section{Business Cases}\label{business-cases}}

\hypertarget{holaluz-supplier-access-and-security}{%
\subsection{Holaluz: Supplier Access and Security}\label{holaluz-supplier-access-and-security}}

\textbf{Case discussed in class:} attackers used an external sales provider's credentials to access Holaluz customer data in \textbf{October 2022}. A shared account used by around \textbf{120 people} and excessive permissions were central security weaknesses.

The class case describes exposure of names, ID numbers, addresses and bank account details, affecting \textbf{more than 31,000 customers}. It reports a \textbf{€675,000 fine} and a \textbf{three-month deadline} for security improvements, including ending shared access and introducing MFA.

The reported total fine concerned \textbf{Articles 5(1)(f) and 32 GDPR}, covering confidentiality and appropriate security measures. \textbf{Study note:} the exact customer count and specific wording of the MFA order in this class case still need confirmation.

\textbf{Management lessons:}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Give each user an individual account.
\item
  Apply \textbf{least privilege}: access only to what the job requires.
\item
  Use \textbf{multi-factor authentication (MFA)} and monitor access.
\item
  Check supplier security in practice, rather than relying only on contracts.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{laliga-biometric-access-and-the-dpia}{%
\subsection{LaLiga: Biometric Access and the DPIA}\label{laliga-biometric-access-and-the-dpia}}

LaLiga promoted biometric identification for access to \textbf{supporters' sections} in football stadiums.

In procedure \textbf{PS/00484/2023}, the AEPD imposed a \textbf{€1 million fine for infringement of Article 35 GDPR}, concerning the required DPIA. It also ordered suspension of the relevant biometric processing until a valid DPIA had been completed successfully.

The specific €1 million sanction concerned \textbf{Article 35}, rather than Articles 6 and 9 or transparency failures. Lawfulness and special-category conditions remain important when analysing biometric processing.

\textbf{Management lesson:} a security objective does not remove data protection duties. Check necessity, less intrusive alternatives, the legal basis and high-risk assessment \textbf{before implementation}.

\hypertarget{november-2026-intellectual-property-and-cybersecurity}{%
\chapter{ Intellectual Property and Cybersecurity}\label{november-2026-intellectual-property-and-cybersecurity}}

\hypertarget{protecting-business-assets}{%
\section{Protecting Business Assets}\label{protecting-business-assets}}

Businesses should identify and protect:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  New products, services and technologies.
\item
  Confidential know-how held by key employees.
\item
  Marketing strategies and business plans.
\item
  Accounting records and financial information.
\item
  Websites, domain names and social media accounts.
\item
  Growth and expansion plans.
\end{itemize}

These assets need different tools: \textbf{IP rights, contracts, confidentiality rules, access controls and cybersecurity}. Not all business information qualifies for an IP right.

\textbf{Know-how} means practical knowledge and skills. A business can protect confidential information, but it does not own employees or all their general experience.

\hypertarget{intellectual-property-and-protection-methods}{%
\section{Intellectual Property and Protection Methods}\label{intellectual-property-and-protection-methods}}

\hypertarget{definition-and-business-purpose}{%
\subsection{Definition and business purpose}\label{definition-and-business-purpose}}

\textbf{Intellectual property (IP)} concerns creations such as inventions, original works, designs and distinctive business signs.

\textbf{IP rights} protect qualifying creations and allow owners to control certain uses, earn income and defend a competitive advantage.

Protection can support innovation, investment, licensing and market differentiation. However, \textbf{not every idea or creation is automatically protected}: each right has its own requirements.

\hypertarget{trade-secrets}{%
\subsection{Trade secrets}\label{trade-secrets}}

A \textbf{trade secret} is information that:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Is \textbf{secret}, rather than generally known or easily available.
\item
  Has \textbf{commercial value because it is secret}.
\item
  Is protected through \textbf{reasonable steps to keep it secret}.
\end{enumerate}

Examples include a confidential production method, supplier list or launch strategy.

An \textbf{NDA --- non-disclosure agreement} --- requires the receiving party to keep defined information confidential. Restricted access and employee training also help protect secrets.

\textbf{Link:} patents protect disclosed inventions for a limited period; trade secrets depend on continued secrecy.

\hypertarget{main-intellectual-property-rights}{%
\section{Main Intellectual Property Rights}\label{main-intellectual-property-rights}}

\hypertarget{patents}{%
\subsection{Patents}\label{patents}}

A \textbf{patent} gives its owner the right to prevent specified unauthorised uses of a protected invention in the territory covered.

The invention must meet three central requirements:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Novelty:} it is new compared with information already publicly available.
\item
  \textbf{Inventive step:} it is not obvious to a person with relevant technical knowledge.
\item
  \textbf{Industrial applicability:} it can be made or used in an industry.
\end{itemize}

It must also be a qualifying technical invention. A patent application must explain the invention sufficiently.

\textbf{Duration:} generally \textbf{20 years from filing}, subject to required fees and legal conditions. A patent can be sold or licensed. It does not automatically give its owner permission to use the invention if other rights or laws prevent this.

\hypertarget{patent-exclusions-and-limits}{%
\subsection{Patent exclusions and limits}\label{patent-exclusions-and-limits}}

Under the European patent framework, exclusions include scientific theories, mathematical methods, aesthetic creations, presentations of information and computer programs \textbf{as such}.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  A \textbf{software-based technical invention} may be patentable if it meets the relevant conditions; not all software is excluded.
\item
  The human body and the simple discovery of its elements are not patentable. Certain isolated elements obtained through a technical process may qualify under specific conditions.
\item
  An aesthetic feature may receive \textbf{design protection}, even when it is not a patentable invention.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{patent-licences}{%
\subsection{Patent licences}\label{patent-licences}}

A \textbf{licence} allows another party to use an IP right under agreed or legally established conditions.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Voluntary or contractual licence:} the owner agrees to permit use. Terms can cover payment, duration, territory and scope. It may be exclusive or non-exclusive.
\item
  \textbf{Compulsory licence:} a competent authority permits certain uses without the owner's consent in legally defined circumstances. Conditions and remuneration depend on the applicable rules.
\end{itemize}

``compulsory licence'' is the usual term, rather than ``mandatory licence''. It is an exceptional legal mechanism, not automatic permission to copy a patent.

\hypertarget{utility-models}{%
\subsection{Utility models}\label{utility-models}}

A \textbf{utility model} protects qualifying technical inventions, often practical improvements to products or devices. Its requirements and availability depend on the country.

In Spain, protection normally lasts \textbf{10 years from filing}. The inventive-step standard is lower than for patents, but the innovation must still meet legal requirements.

\textbf{Example:} an improved shoe fastening mechanism may qualify; a purely decorative change does not become a technical invention just because it is useful commercially.

\hypertarget{industrial-designs}{%
\subsection{Industrial designs}\label{industrial-designs}}

An \textbf{industrial design} protects a product's \textbf{appearance}, such as shape, lines, colours, patterns or decoration. It does not protect a technical function as such.

For a registered design, \textbf{novelty and individual character} are central requirements. Registered protection in Spain and the EU generally starts with \textbf{5 years}, renewable up to \textbf{25 years}.

\textbf{Example:} a distinctive bottle shape or the visible pattern of a running shoe.

\hypertarget{trademarks-brands-and-domain-names}{%
\subsection{Trademarks, brands and domain names}\label{trademarks-brands-and-domain-names}}

A \textbf{trademark} is a distinctive sign that identifies the commercial origin of goods or services. It may be a name, logo or another qualifying sign.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  It helps customers recognise a business and protects brand identity.
\item
  Registration concerns specified goods or services and a defined territory.
\item
  In Spain and the EU, registration generally lasts \textbf{10 years} and can be renewed repeatedly, subject to requirements including genuine use.
\end{itemize}

A \textbf{brand} is broader: it includes reputation, values and customer perceptions. A trademark legally protects qualifying signs used within that brand.

A \textbf{domain name} is a registered internet address. Registering one does \textbf{not automatically create a trademark right} or allow infringement of another company's trademark.

\hypertarget{copyright-economic-and-moral-rights}{%
\subsection{Copyright: Economic and Moral Rights}\label{copyright-economic-and-moral-rights}}

\textbf{Copyright} protects original expression, including books, photographs, music, films and software code. It does not protect an idea simply because someone thought of it.

\textbf{Economic rights} control commercial uses such as copying, distribution and adaptation. They may be licensed or transferred under applicable rules.

\textbf{Moral rights} protect the author's personal connection with the work, including recognition of authorship and the work's integrity.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Economic rights:} the general Spanish rule is the author's life plus \textbf{70 years after death}, with exceptions and transitional rules.
\item
  \textbf{Moral rights:} In Spain, authorship and integrity rights can be exercised after death \textbf{without a time limit} by the persons authorised under the law.
\item
  Original works normally receive copyright \textbf{without registration}.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{cybersecurity-and-its-link-to-data-protection}{%
\section{Cybersecurity and Its Link to Data Protection}\label{cybersecurity-and-its-link-to-data-protection}}

\textbf{Cybersecurity} protects networks, systems and data against threats such as unauthorised access, malware, theft and disruption.

Three main objectives are:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Confidentiality:} only authorised people can access information.
\item
  \textbf{Integrity:} information remains correct and is not changed without permission.
\item
  \textbf{Availability:} authorised users can access systems and data when needed.
\end{itemize}

These are known as the \textbf{CIA triad}.

Practical measures include individual accounts, least privilege, MFA, encryption, updates, backups, access logs, training, regular checks and incident response plans.

\textbf{Data protection} asks whether personal data is used lawfully and fairly. \textbf{Cybersecurity} asks how systems and information are protected. They overlap, but cybersecurity also protects non-personal information and business operations.
